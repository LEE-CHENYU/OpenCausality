Venezuelan Oil Supply Unlock: Causal Transmission to Global Oil Prices

When the United States lifts sanctions on Venezuela, the immediate effect is not a
flood of oil but a reopening of export channels and a signal to international capital.
In the short term (weeks to months), existing wells that were throttled or shut-in due
to lack of buyers can restart, adding roughly 100-150 thousand barrels per day to
Venezuelan output. But the larger effect operates through investment: sanctions relief
removes the legal barrier that has kept Chevron, Repsol, ENI, and other international
operators from deploying capital into well workovers, upgrader repairs (especially the
Petropiar facility), and Orinoco Belt rehabilitation. This FDI channel is the mechanism
through which production climbs from ~900 thousand bpd today to a potential 1.2 million
bpd by end-2026 and 2 million bpd within two years.

Venezuelan production feeds mechanically into global oil supply: every additional barrel
produced adds to the ~108.6 million bpd world total. But this is not the only supply
channel. OPEC+ spare capacity — currently a historically high 5.8 million bpd — means
Saudi Arabia and other Gulf producers can strategically offset or amplify the Venezuelan
increase. OPEC's reaction function is endogenous and strategic: they may cut quotas to
defend prices, or they may allow the surplus to build to discipline higher-cost producers.
Venezuela is exempt from OPEC+ quotas, which means its production increases are not
constrained by group agreements but will nonetheless affect group dynamics.

Non-OPEC supply growth — driven by the United States, Guyana, Brazil, and Canada —
also adds to global supply independently of Venezuelan dynamics. The IEA already projects
world output rising by 2.4 million bpd in 2026, creating a potential surplus of nearly
4 million bpd even before accounting for a Venezuelan unlock.

The supply-demand balance determines the direction and magnitude of price movements.
Global oil demand, driven by economic growth (particularly in China, India, and
Southeast Asia), seasonal patterns, and the pace of the energy transition, sets the
floor. With short-run demand elasticity estimated at only -0.05, even modest supply
increases can produce outsized price effects: a 400 thousand bpd Venezuelan increase
(0.37% of global supply) implies roughly a 7% price decline — about $4/bbl off a $58
Brent baseline — absent any OPEC offset.

However, the relationship between supply and price is moderated by two critical factors.
First, OPEC's strategic response: if Saudi Arabia cuts production by the same amount
Venezuela adds, the net effect on global supply — and therefore price — is zero. Second,
the type of crude matters. Venezuelan heavy sour crude is not fungible with US light
tight oil. US Gulf Coast refineries were specifically engineered for Venezuelan heavy
crude and operate more efficiently with it than with domestic light sweet barrels. This
creates a heavy-sour crude discount channel: increased Venezuelan supply specifically
lowers the premium on heavy sour grades, benefiting US refiners' margins even if
headline Brent prices don't move much.

The feedback loop from price to investment closes the system: lower oil prices reduce
the return on Venezuelan upstream investment, slowing the production recovery. This
creates a natural speed limit — Venezuelan production cannot grow indefinitely because
the price impact of its own growth eventually chokes off the investment that drives
further growth. At current forecasts ($58 Brent), the economics of Orinoco Belt heavy
oil are marginal, meaning the investment case depends critically on whether prices
stabilize above $50-55/bbl.

The key identification challenge is distinguishing the effect of Venezuelan supply
specifically from the broader non-OPEC supply wave. The sanctions-relief event provides
a quasi-natural experiment — a sharp, politically determined supply shock that is
plausibly exogenous to oil market fundamentals — but the OPEC reaction function and
simultaneous non-OPEC growth confound the reduced-form relationship between Venezuelan
production and Brent prices. A credible estimate of the price impact requires either
instrumenting for the sanctions shock or structurally modeling the OPEC reaction.
